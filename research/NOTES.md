# Research notes: intake form design

Running findings on what makes a good apartment intake form for SF renters. Add a bullet when you
learn something; keep each one short and say where it came from.

The reviewer agent reads this whole file as context every time it proposes a new form version,
so write findings as guidance it can act on. Keep the file short (it is sent with every proposal).

## Findings

- Every answer is optional. A skip is signal (the question was unclear, too personal, or didn't
  matter to this renter), not missing data to nag about. (brainstorm 2026-10-17)
- When a renter rewrites a question before answering, that is stronger signal than the answer:
  the rewrite shows what they actually care about and how they'd phrase it. (brainstorm)
- Ask how negotiable a preference is, not just what it is. Some renters are price-elastic, some
  location-elastic; weighting every preference equally and treating it as absolute is wrong. (brainstorm)
- Don't let the most vocal or most opinionated renters dictate the form. Judge changes on the
  acceptance and held-out batches, not on the loudest feedback. (brainstorm)
- For known fields (budget, bedrooms, move-in, pets), a form beats open-ended chat: renters can
  see how many questions are left, and the answers are structured. (brainstorm)
- Show how much each answer narrows the options ("212 listings left"), so renters can tell when
  they are being too specific or too vague. (brainstorm)
- Subjective details matter (floor, layout, which side of Market), but asking about every one is
  taxing. Prefer one open question ("what did you hate about your last place?") over many narrow
  ones. (brainstorm)
